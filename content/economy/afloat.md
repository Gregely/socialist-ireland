**The budget.** Ireland today against the republic at the end of the transition and in the long run. Every euro of revenue and spending is accounted for; a deficit that savers and the Central Bank's voted ceiling cannot finance is shown as an *unfinanced gap*, never filled by assumption.

**The external balance.** What the domestic economy earns in foreign currency and what it must pay out. This is where the design is most exposed: the multinationals' local spending is the largest single inflow, and energy, food and medicines cannot be cut quickly.

**Investment.** Public investment is planned (Art. 56), financed first by the Social Wealth Fund's returns and then by borrowing from savers through the public bank. Money creation by the Central Bank is a last resort, capped by a ceiling the Assembly votes each year (Art. 61.4). Enterprises invest from their own surplus; co-operatives' reserves are indivisible, so most of their surplus stays invested (Art. 50.3). **Soft budgets are the classic failure here (FM-06):** Kornai's "investment hunger". The design's answer is hard budgets (Art. 54), open books (Art. 59) and local control of a third of capital spending (Art. 57). In the model, the waste in public investment falls as participation in planning rises. That link is the drafter's assumption, and the weakest in the model.

**The transition is where it is most likely to break.** Years 0–2 bring the notice period and the run on deposits; year 2 brings the new currency; years 2–5 bring the multinationals' departures and the trade terms. After that the model settles towards a long run, if it gets there.
