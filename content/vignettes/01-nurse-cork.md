title: The night shift
title_ga: An seal oíche
who: Áine Ó Murchú, 34, staff nurse, Cork University Hospital

Áine gets the 208 bus at half seven. It's free, like every public bus since the second year of the transition, and it's late, like every 208 since before the transition. The new electric fleet was supposed to arrive in spring. The county council blames the National Investment Council, the Investment Council blames the supplier, and the supplier is a co-op in Ballinasloe with a strike on. Áine reads the rota on her phone on the way in.

The rota is new this month. The ward's [[workplace-councils|workplace council]] spent three meetings, two of them very long and one of them very bad-tempered, agreeing how the night shifts and the worst jobs get shared out. Before, the newest nurses got every Saturday night. Now everyone takes their turn, including the clinical nurse manager, who complained about it in the meeting and then did her first Saturday last week without complaining at all. Áine voted for the new rota. She also missed a friend's fortieth because of it.

At midnight the ward is quiet enough to eat. The talk is about Fiachra, the registrar who left for Boston in August. The consultants' pay is capped at four times the lowest wage in the hospital (Art. 55), and Fiachra said so in his leaving speech, not unkindly. Half the room thinks he was right to go. The other half points out that the waiting list for his clinic, which used to be eighteen months, is now eleven weeks, and nobody gets seen faster for paying any more (Art. 20). Both halves are correct, which is why the conversation goes round in circles until a call bell ends it.

At three, an elderly man on the ward asks Áine whether the hospital is "a Government place now". She says it always was. He says it didn't use to feel like it. She doesn't know if he means that as a complaint.

At half four there's a quiet half-hour, and Áine fills in the time-use diary from the [[care-service|National Care Service]] survey (Art. 71). It asks how many hours last week she spent caring for her mother, who has dementia and lives in Mayfield. Eleven, she writes, then crosses it out and writes fourteen, because she has been rounding down for years. Her mother gets three home-care visits a day now. The carers are well paid and they stay; it is the same two women most days. Áine still does the shopping, the Sunday, the worrying. Her brother in Mallow does the bins and the phone calls and thinks that is half.

Her shift ends at eight. On the way out she passes a poster for the [[local-assemblies|local assembly]] in Bishopstown: the participatory budget vote is on Thursday, swimming pool versus a new junction at the Wilton roundabout. She will not go. She'll be asleep. She feels faintly guilty about this, then remembers that there is a substitute-care allowance for her mother if she ever *does* get drawn by lot for something, and that she has never once been drawn for anything in her life.

The 208 is on time going home.
