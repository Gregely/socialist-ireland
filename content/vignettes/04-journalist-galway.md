Róisín has been a thorn in the side of every Irish government since 2009, and she sees no reason to stop now. Her co-operative, *An Tuairisceoir Neamhspleách*, is funded mostly by readers' media vouchers: every adult gets a sum each year to direct to the news outlets of their choice (Art. 79). Last year 41,000 people chose hers. She knows the number because the [[media-regulator|Media Regulator]] publishes it, and because she checks it more often than is healthy.

This morning she does something she has meant to do for two years. She requests her own file from the [[intelligence|intelligence service]] (Art. 16).

It arrives at eleven: four pages. One records her attendance at a protest against a data-centre charter in 2029. Another is a note from a warrant application, refused by a judge, to monitor her phone during the wind-farm story. She reads the refusal three times. The judge wrote "no evidence of anything but journalism". She frames that page. She also sends the whole file to her lawyer, because the warrant application should never have been made, and the [[security-oversight|Security Oversight Committee]] ought to know who made it.

:::variant Q1=A
In the afternoon she writes the piece she is actually paid to write: an analysis of the last election. The party won 61% of the seats in the [[national-assembly|National Assembly]] against independent candidates, many of whom ran on a programme that was, in everything but name, a party's programme. Her argument is that the leading party wins not because the law guarantees it — it doesn't — but because it is the only organisation allowed to organise, and organisation beats individuals every time. Her headline is "The Monopoly That Isn't Written Down". She expects a reply.

The reply comes at four, not from the State but from a party official in Galway, who rings her editor to say the piece is "unhelpful at a sensitive time". Her editor, who is elected by the co-op's members and is not afraid of anyone, tells him the piece runs on Thursday. It's not an offence to make a phone call. It's not an offence to be unhelpful. Róisín notes the call in her diary and in the piece. An internal platform in the party, the one that opposes the data-centre deals, shares the article on its own channels. Under Article 31 it can, and nobody can stop it. She finds that more interesting than the phone call.
:::

:::variant Q1=B
In the afternoon she writes the piece she is actually paid to write: an analysis of the last election. Four socialist parties won seats. The court refused registration to a fifth, the Social Market Alliance, on the grounds that its programme called for privatising the grid. Róisín thinks the court was legally right and politically wrong, and says so: 140,000 people voted for independents who ran on the Alliance's programme anyway. Her headline is "The Line We Drew". A minister calls it "irresponsible". Under Article 10, he's entitled to his view.
:::

:::variant Q1=D
In the afternoon she writes the piece she is actually paid to write: an analysis of the last election. No party names were on the ballot, but she has spent three months mapping which candidates shared donors, canvassers and press releases. She finds three informal slates, one of which won 58% of the seats. Her headline is "The Parties That Aren't There". Nobody denies it. That is what worries her.
:::

At six she goes to the pub with two people from the wind-farm campaign. One of them says she should be grateful: in other countries, at other times, a reporter like her would be in prison. Róisín says she knows, and that "you're not in prison" is a low bar for a republic. They drink to the low bar anyway.
