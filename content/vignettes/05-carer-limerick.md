Tomás has had MS for twenty-two years. Bríd gave up her job on the line at a medical-devices plant in Raheen to care for him in 2006, long before the transition, and before anybody paid her a cent for it. Now the [[care-service|National Care Service]] pays her a carer's income (Art. 21.3). It's less than she earned in Raheen and more than she ever expected to see. The first payment came in a brown envelope, and she cried in the kitchen, and was furious with herself for crying.

The home-care worker, a young man called Eoin from Corbally, comes at nine to help get Tomás up and showered. Then Bríd has three hours. On Tuesdays she spends them on the standing committee of the Moyross [[local-assemblies|local assembly]], to which she was drawn by lot in January (Art. 35.3). While she's there, Eoin's colleague stays with Tomás. That's the substitute-care allowance (Art. 29.2). Without it she couldn't go. She has worked out that most of the women her age on the estate couldn't have gone either, before.

The committee prepares the assembly's business. This month that means the participatory budget: Moyross's share of a third of the county's capital spending (Art. 57). There are two proposals left. One is resurfacing the footpaths on the Watch House Cross side. The other is a community laundry and food hall in the old parish hall, with a hot dinner at cost every weekday. The young fellas on the committee want the footpaths, because their mothers trip on them. Bríd wants the food hall, because her Tuesdays and Thursdays would get a whole hour back, and so would a lot of people's.

The officer from the [[county-councils|city and county council]] brings costings. The footpaths are cheaper. The food hall's running costs would fall on the council, and the council's budget is under pressure because of the shortfall from the multinationals leaving. The officer says this gently, as if he expects to be shouted at. Nobody shouts.

They agree to put both to the full assembly on Thursday, with the costings printed in large type. Bríd votes to recommend the food hall. She loses, four to three. She says she'll argue for it at the assembly anyway. The chair, who is nineteen, says that is fair.

At home, Eoin has Tomás in the chair by the window with the radio on. There's a time-use diary on the table (Art. 71). Bríd fills it in honestly for the first time in her life: seventy-one hours of care last week. Writing the number down makes her angrier than anything has in years. It isn't the number itself. It's that nobody ever asked before.

On Thursday the assembly votes for the food hall, 112 to 98. The footpaths go on next year's list. One of the young fellas from the committee comes up to her afterwards and says his mam told him to vote for it.
