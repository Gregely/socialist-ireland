The letter came eighteen months ago, and Tunde nearly binned it with the takeaway menus. *You have been selected by lot to serve in the Citizens' Chamber.* He has lived in Tallaght for nineteen years and been a citizen for eleven. He rang the number twice to make sure it wasn't a scam.

His job at the bus depot is held for him (Art. 29). His pay continues. The lads at the depot call him "Senator" and leave copies of the *Dáil Debates* on his old bench, which is funnier than it should be.

Today is the [[pay-panel|Pay and Privileges Panel]]: twenty-one members of the Chamber, drawn by lot again, for one year. The question on the agenda is whether members of the [[executive-council|Executive Council]] can have the use of a state car. The secretariat's briefing pack is seventy pages. Tunde reads it on the Luas, and in the evening at the kitchen table while his daughter does her Irish homework across from him. It recommends yes, on security grounds.

Tunde notices something. The pack cites a security assessment. He finds the actual assessment on the [[open-data|open-data site]], which is where everything ends up eventually. It says a car is advisable *for the Chair of the Council during periods of elevated threat*, not for all seven members all the time. He's not sure whether the secretariat made a mistake or whether somebody wanted the yes. He spends an hour being unsure and then decides it doesn't matter, because either way the pack is wrong.

At the session, a former senior civil servant gives evidence for the cars. She is very good. Tunde finds himself agreeing with her, then catches himself. He asks his question about the assessment. There's a pause. She says that is a fair reading. A woman from Ballina, a retired primary teacher, says the Council members can get the bus like everyone else, and the room laughs, and then realises she isn't joking.

The Panel votes 15–6: a pooled car for the Chair only, logged and published. It's binding (Art. 100). Nobody can overturn it but a later Panel.

On the way out a man from the secretariat, friendly, the kind of friendly that makes you count your fingers, mentions that the [[security-oversight|Security Oversight Committee]] will need new members drawn next month, "if he's interested". Tunde knows that members are drawn by lot, not invited. He says so. The man says of course, of course, he only meant that Tunde would be good at it. This is the kind of thing Tunde has learned to write down. He emails it, with the time and the man's name, to the Chamber's co-chairs, who hire and rotate the staff themselves (Art. 39.3).

He gets home at eight. His daughter asks what he did today. He says he stopped seven people getting a car. She asks if that's a good thing. He says he thinks so. She says that's a weird job. He says it is, and that in six months it's over and someone else gets the letter.
