The cows don't know about the land-use plan. Pádraig feeds them at seven anyway: forty-one sucklers, down from fifty-six three years ago. Leitrim land is wet and heavy. Ask anyone in the county and they'll say it was never really cattle land, and then they'll say they'd never sell the cattle.

The fifteen cows that went were not taken. He took the [[land-farm-board|Board's]] offer for them, because the income guarantee pays him his average of the five years before the transition (Art. 67), and those were good years for weanlings. He gets a stewardship payment for the twelve acres down at the river that are being let go back to wet grassland, and another for the four acres of birch and alder the Coillte crew planted in March. On paper he is better off than he was. On the road he is a man with fewer cattle than his father had.

At eleven he drives to Carrick for the regional meeting of the Land and Farm Transition Board. Half its members are elected by farmers. Pádraig was elected two years ago, mostly because nobody else in the electoral area would stand. The agenda is forty pages. Item six is the [[ecological-authority|Ecological Authority's]] annual review of the nitrogen budget, which says the north-west is on track and the south-east is not. Item seven is the price the dairy co-op will pay for a new cheese line in Manorhamilton. Item nine is a complaint from a farmer in Kiltyclogher that the forestry crew left a gate open.

Item nine takes the longest.

The Authority's representative, a soil scientist from Galway, sits at the end of the table without a vote and is polite to everyone. Pádraig doesn't dislike her. He dislikes that she can veto things. A man from Ballinamore says what Pádraig half-thinks: that Dublin people who have never pulled a calf are deciding what happens to land they've never stood on. The chair, a woman who milks ninety cows near Mohill, reminds him that the budgets are set by the Assembly in Dublin, which the Ballinamore man voted for, and that the Authority enforces them. The Ballinamore man says that's the same thing. It isn't, quite, and everyone at the table knows it isn't, and it doesn't help.

On the way home Pádraig stops at the co-op mart. It's a co-op again, as it was when his father sold there; the company that bought it in the 1990s had to sell it back to its members and workers under the transition law (Art. 67.3). The canteen is better. There are fewer cattle in the pens than there were.

His son, Cian, comes in at six from the [[job-guarantee|Job Guarantee]] retrofit crew. They are insulating council houses in Carrigallen. He's twenty-four and says he'll never farm. He says it every week, and Pádraig says nothing every week.

After the dinner Pádraig reads the [[open-data|published accounts]] of the new cheese line on the laptop. He doesn't fully understand them. He is fairly sure the man from Ballinamore doesn't either. The man from Ballinamore will be at the meeting next month regardless, and so will Pádraig, because if they don't go, somebody else will decide.
