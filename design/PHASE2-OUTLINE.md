**Status:** draft for review. **Nothing in Part A has been decided.** Each question sets out 2–4 options, scores them against the failure modes they affect, and lists what each one costs. Part B sketches the Phase 2 deliverables (`constitution.md` and `institutions.json`), with `[Q#]` marking each place where your decision plugs in.

## How to read the scores

The scores are **reasoned judgements from the research**, not measurements. Every score has a one-line reason, and you should argue with them.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `++` | strongly resists the failure mode (good) |
| `+` | resists it |
| `0` | neutral, or effects cancel out |
| `−` | makes it more likely |
| `−−` | strongly makes it more likely |
| `?` | evidence too thin to say |

# Part A — The open questions

## Q1 · The party question

*Who may organise to contest political power, and on what terms?*

| | **A. Leading party with internal democracy** | **B. Socialist pluralism** | **C. Open pluralism** | **D. No-party council system** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | One party leads the state. It is constitutionally required to allow internal platforms and factions, elect its leaders, and observe term limits. Other organisations exist but do not contest national power | Any number of parties may contest elections if they accept the constitution's core (democratic rights plus social ownership). A court decides who falls outside the line | Any party may stand, including pro-capitalist ones, under ordinary democratic law. Socialism has to keep winning | Candidates stand as individuals or mandated delegates. Parties may exist as associations but get no ballot labels and no public funding. Heavy use of sortition |
| **Precedent** | Bolsheviks before the 1921 ban; Yugoslav LCY; Cuba's "superior leading force" ([02](../research/02-suppression-of-pluralism.md), [06](../research/06-positive-models.md)) | Soviets in 1917 (several socialist parties); Kronstadt's demand for freedom for "left socialist parties and anarchists" ([02](../research/02-suppression-of-pluralism.md)); German "militant democracy" party bans (*not yet sourced*) | All liberal democracies; Chile 1970–73 ([05](../research/05-counter-revolution-and-external-pressure.md)) | Athens; Uganda's "Movement" system 1986–2005 ([FES](https://collections.fes.de/publikationen/download/ftpack/plain/447697); [Ciganikova](https://stichproben.univie.ac.at/fileadmin/user_upload/p_stichproben/Artikel/Nummer15/Nr15_Ciganikova.pdf)); Rojava's councils alongside a dominant PYD ([06](../research/06-positive-models.md)) |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-01 new class | `−−` party controls appointments (Voslensky, Michels) | `+` rivals expose privilege | `+` rivals expose privilege | `+` no party machine controls nominations, but `?` Michels predicts informal machines |
| FM-02 monopoly | `−−` the 1918–21 sequence is this option's history | `+` real plurality, **but** the line on what counts as "socialist" can be used to exclude (the June 1918 expulsions were justified as anti-counter-revolutionary) | `++` | `−` Uganda's no-party system became one-party in practice; Rojava is similar |
| FM-03 cult | `−` cults grew in single-party systems | `+` | `+` | `0` personalist politics replaces party politics (Uganda) |
| FM-04 security capture | `−` Cheka as "direct agency of the Party" | `+` opposition sits on oversight bodies | `+` | `0` |
| FM-10 counter-revolution | `+` cohesion in a crisis, short term | `+` excludes parties of open restoration | `−` legal channel for funding and media campaigns (Chile) | `−` slow to coordinate under attack |
| FM-12 restoration from inside | `−−` 1991 was carried out by the party elite | `+` | `−` restoration by ballot is possible, though legitimate | `0` |
| FM-13 splits | `−` disagreement becomes schism or purge | `++` splits become new parties | `++` | `+` no parties to split; informal factions remain |
| FM-14 nationalism | `−` Yugoslavia: national capture of the party's republic-level branches | `0` | `0` nationalist parties can form | `0` |
| FM-17 legitimacy | `−−` ritualised participation (Yurchak) | `+` real choice, but excluded voters resent it | `++` freely chosen and reversible | `?` |

**What each costs.**

- **A.**
  - Probably unwinnable by referendum in Ireland.
  - Incompatible with the ECHR's guarantees on free elections and association (*legal sourcing needed*).
  - Incompatible with EU membership (Art. 2 TEU values).
  - No historical case of a single ruling party staying internally democratic over a generation.
- **B.**
  - Excludes the constituency of every current major Irish party unless they re-found themselves.
  - Needs a body to police the boundary, and that body becomes a powerful political actor (FM-01, FM-04).
  - Likely to conflict with ECHR case law, which allows party bans only in narrow circumstances (*legal sourcing needed*).
- **C.**
  - Socialism can be voted out.
  - Capital can back the opposition and threaten flight before elections.
  - Every election is a referendum on the system.
- **D.**
  - Parties re-form informally (slates, caucuses), with *less* transparency.
  - The best-organised existing network gains (unions, church, or a party under another name).
  - Voters lose the shortcut that party programmes provide, which raises the cost of participating (FM-08).

**Strongest objection to each.**

- **A:** the whole of file 02.
- **B:** "socialist" will be defined by whoever holds the court.
- **C:** Chile 1973.
- **D:** Uganda and Rojava show that "no parties" means one hidden party.

**Interactions.**

- If you choose C, Q2 (entrenchment) and Q5 (sortition powers) carry most of the anti-restoration weight.
- If you choose B, you need a very robust and independent constitutional court (Q6).
- If you choose D, you need strong sortition powers (Q5 C or D).

## Q2 · Entrenchment vs reversibility of social ownership

*How hard should it be for a future majority to privatise social property?*

| | **A. Ordinary law** | **B. In the constitution, amendable by simple-majority referendum** | **C. Heightened protection** | **D. Eternity clause** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | Social ownership is set by statute. Any parliamentary majority can reverse it | As Art. 46 of the current Bunreacht: a Bill, then a referendum | A two-thirds assembly vote **and** a referendum, **or** two referendums at least two years apart and across a general election | The core of social ownership cannot be amended at all |
| **Precedent** | UK nationalisations reversed by later governments (*not sourced in this pass*) | Bunreacht Art. 46 ([08](../research/08-ireland-today.md)) | Double passage across an election is common in European constitutions (*sourcing needed*) | German Basic Law Art. 79(3): Arts. 1 and 20 (dignity, democracy, the social state, the rule of law, federalism) cannot be amended ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Law_for_the_Federal_Republic_of_Germany); [Katholische Akademie Bayern](https://kath-akademie-bayern.de/en/mediathek-eintrag/selbstschutzmechanismen-des-grundgesetzes-zum-politischen-system-der-wehrhaften-demokratie/)) |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-12 restoration from inside | `−−` | `−` | `+` an elite cannot privatise quietly; it must win twice in public | `++` on paper; `?` in practice. The Soviet constitution entrenched socialism and the system was dismantled anyway |
| FM-02 monopoly | `++` | `+` | `0` | `−−` the people cannot change their minds lawfully, so change can only come through rupture |
| FM-10 counter-revolution | `−−` one election undoes everything | `−` | `+` | `+` |
| FM-11 capital flight | `−` investors bet on reversal and may strike to force it | `0` | `+` a credible commitment reduces the incentive to wait it out | `+` |
| FM-15 emergency powers | `0` | `0` | `0` | `−` enforcing an unamendable clause against a determined majority may need coercion |
| FM-17 legitimacy | `+` fully accountable | `+` | `0` | `−−` "you can't vote on this" breeds cynicism |

**What each costs.**

- **A:** no protection at all. Everything rides on every election.
- **B:** the same majority that created social ownership can undo it. This is the honest democratic baseline.
- **C:**
  - Asymmetry: the transition itself may have needed only simple majorities, yet reversal needs a supermajority. Opponents will call this rigging, with some justice.
  - **One mitigation:** the transition itself must clear the same heightened bar.
- **D:** turns a political settlement into a sacred text, and history suggests such texts don't hold.

**Note:** different parts can be entrenched differently. For example, rights could be eternal, social ownership heightened, and economic details ordinary. Part B assumes some such tiering. *Which tier each thing goes in is part of your decision.*

**Interactions.** Q1 C plus Q2 A is the most reversible combination. Q1 A plus Q2 D is the least reversible.

## Q3 · The North

*How does a socialist Ireland relate to the six counties and to unity?*

**The binding constraints apply to every option.**

- Bunreacht Art. 3 requires consent *"in both jurisdictions in the island"*.
- The GFA protects parity of esteem and the right to be British, Irish or both.
- A border poll is in the Secretary of State's hands (NI Act 1998, Sched. 1; *McCord*, 2020) ([08 §11](../research/08-ireland-today.md)).
- Current Northern polling: about 34–36% for unity and 42–48% for the status quo ([08](../research/08-ireland-today.md)).

| | **A. 26 counties first** | **B. Unity first, socialism later** | **C. Combined all-island convention** | **D. Federal / confederal Ireland** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | A socialist republic in the South, with the constitution keeping the door open to unity by consent | Pursue unity under a pluralist all-island constitution; the economic system is decided by later democratic politics | One all-island constitutional process puts both questions on the table, with consociational guarantees (cross-community vetoes on identity provisions) | Unity as a federation or confederation: the North has wide autonomy, possibly including over its economic model within shared limits |
| **Precedent** | The current Art. 3 position | The Shared Island / ARINS approach puts unity first (*not sourced in this pass*) | The GFA itself was a negotiated, consociational, dual-referendum settlement | Belgium, Switzerland; "one country, two systems" as a cautionary case (*not sourced*) |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-14 nationalism | `+` socialism is not tied to the national question; but `−` a system border on the island can harden identities on both sides | `−−` socialism is subordinated to the national question, the reverse of Connolly's 1897 warning ([07](../research/07-irish-lineage.md)) | `+` civic, with both identities protected | `+` |
| FM-10 counter-revolution | `−` a hostile neighbour across a land border; fear of loyalist mobilisation | `0` | `0` | `0` |
| FM-11 capital flight | `−` a smaller economy; capital can move North | `?` the North's fiscal position depends on UK transfers (*not sourced*) | `?` | `−` capital can move between the two systems inside one state |
| FM-13 splits | `0` | `−` the left divides over priorities | `−` a complex coalition | `0` |
| FM-17 legitimacy | `+` in the South; `−` Northern socialists are shut out | `+` if unity passes | `++` if it passes: the broadest legitimacy | `+` |
| FM-02 pluralism | `0` | `+` | `+` vetoes protect minorities | `+` |

**What each costs.**

- **A:** economic and system differences on the island have to be managed under the GFA's open border and the Windsor Framework. Socialists in the North are left out.
- **B:** may never reach socialism, and the left spends a generation on the constitutional question.
- **C:**
  - Needs majorities in both jurisdictions for two large changes at once, so it is the **least likely to pass**.
  - Consociational vetoes may block socialist provisions. That is intended, but it waters down the project.
- **D:** two economic systems in one state means transfers, arbitrage and capital flight, plus great complexity.

**Strongest objection to each.**

- **A:** partition made permanent by economics.
- **B:** Connolly's green flag over Dublin Castle.
- **C:** trying to do everything at once means doing nothing.
- **D:** a federation of incompatible systems is unstable.

**Interactions.** Strongly tied to Q4: the North is in the EU single market for goods under the Windsor Framework. Also tied to Q8 (the name) and to how the project reads to unionists.

## Q4 · The EU

*How does a socialist Ireland relate to the Union and its law?* ([08 §10](../research/08-ireland-today.md))

| | **A. Work within** | **B. Negotiated derogations** | **C. Exit** | **D. Selective non-compliance** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | Use the space that exists: Art. 345 (ownership neutrality), SGEI/Altmark, public procurement, cooperatives | Seek treaty protocols or opt-outs for specific instruments (state aid to cooperatives, capital controls, fiscal rules) | Leave the EU, and the euro with it | Stay in, but openly refuse specific rules and accept infringement proceedings |
| **Precedent** | Public ownership of ESB and other state companies today; *Essent* (2013) ([08](../research/08-ireland-today.md)) | Ireland's own Justice and Home Affairs opt-outs; Danish opt-outs (*not sourced*) | Brexit (*costs not sourced*) | Hungary and Poland's rule-of-law disputes are uncomfortable precedents, both from the right; Greece 2015 (*not sourced*) |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-11 blockade / capital flight | `+` single market and euro membership protect against isolation | `0` | `−−` loss of market access; multinationals leave; a new currency invites capital flight | `−` fines, uncertainty, investor flight |
| FM-05 planning | `−` constrains key tools: state aid, capital controls, procurement, competition law on public monopolies | `+` if it succeeds | `+` full toolkit | `+` |
| FM-06 soft budgets | `+` state-aid rules *harden* budget constraints, an underrated safeguard | `0` | `−` | `0` |
| FM-02 monopoly | `+` the EU's rule-of-law framework is an external check on domestic authoritarianism | `+` | `−` the external check is gone | `−` normalises the state ignoring law, a precedent that cuts against our own constitution |
| FM-14 nationalism | `+` | `0` | `−` exit politics tends toward nationalism | `0` |
| FM-16 ecology | `+` an EU climate-law floor | `+` | `?` | `0` |
| FM-17 legitimacy | `0` | `0` | `−` living standards likely fall sharply in the transition | `?` |

**What each costs.**

- **A:** the transition is limited to what EU law allows. Capital controls and large state aid to cooperatives are especially hard.
- **B:** treaty change needs unanimity, so a socialist Ireland's leverage is small. It may take years and fail.
- **C:**
  - The single largest economic shock available.
  - Hardens the border question in new ways.
  - Gives up a check against one's own authoritarian drift.
- **D:** legal and financial attrition, and the precedent of a state that obeys law selectively.

**Note.** ECHR membership comes through the Council of Europe and is **independent** of the EU. Every option can, and in my view should, keep it, but that is also your call.

**Interactions.** Q3: the North's position under the Windsor Framework. Q7: how much planning EU law allows.

## Q5 · Binding sortition

*What powers should randomly selected bodies hold?* ([06 §10–11](../research/06-positive-models.md))

| | **A. Advisory only** | **B. Agenda-setting** | **C. Suspensive veto and oversight** | **D. Co-equal chamber by lot** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | As Ireland's citizens' assemblies now: recommendations that government may ignore | A standing body chosen by lot sets topics for assemblies. The legislature must respond with reasons within a fixed time | A standing chamber chosen by lot can delay legislation, refer it to referendum, trigger audits, set officials' pay and sit on security oversight | A second legislative chamber chosen by lot, with full co-equal powers |
| **Precedent** | Ireland 2012 onward | Ostbelgien's *Bürgerrat* (2019) | Elements of the Athenian *boulē*; no full modern precedent | The Athenian *boulē* (partly); no modern national precedent |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-01 new class | `0` | `+` | `++` officials' pay and audits are in the hands of non-careerists | `++` |
| FM-03 cult | `0` | `0` | `+` a check on the executive | `+` |
| FM-04 security | `0` | `0` | `+` oversight by a body nobody can capture through elections | `+` |
| FM-08 participation | `−` selective follow-through teaches cynicism | `+` a guaranteed response | `+` | `?` members have no constituency to answer to; heavy load |
| FM-12 restoration | `0` | `0` | `+` can refer a privatisation to referendum | `+` |
| FM-13 splits | `0` | `0` | `+` | `+` no party blocs in the chamber |
| FM-17 legitimacy | `−` | `+` | `+` | `?` legitimacy without an electorate is untested at national scale |

**What each costs.**

- **A:** little risk and little gain.
- **B:** modest. The legislature can still say no.
- **C:**
  - Gridlock.
  - **Secretariat capture.** Members chosen by lot depend on staff, so the staff become the real power.
  - Accountability: who removes a member chosen by lot who behaves badly?
- **D:** all the costs of C, magnified. Expertise and continuity are thin, and there is no precedent.

**Strongest objection to C and D.** "Government by whoever answered the phone," in the liberal critique. The left critique: a lot-chosen body can be steered by whoever briefs it.

## Q6 · Separation of powers vs the Commune's fusion

*How are legislative, executive and judicial power arranged?*

| | **A. Classic separation** | **B. Commune fusion** | **C. Collegial hybrid** | **D. Parliamentary (status quo)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it means** | A separately elected executive, a legislature and an independent judiciary | The assembly governs through its own committees (*"executive and legislative at the same time"*); judges and police are elected and recallable | A **collective executive** of about 7–9 people, elected by and recallable by the assembly, with a rotating one-year chair. A **fully independent judiciary and constitutional court**. **Independent integrity bodies** (audit, ombudsman, electoral, ecological, media) insulated from both | A Taoiseach and cabinet drawn from a Dáil majority, with independent courts |
| **Precedent** | US-style presidential systems (*not sourced*) | The Paris Commune ([06 §1](../research/06-positive-models.md)) | The Swiss Federal Council: 7 members elected by the Federal Assembly, a one-year rotating presidency, "first among equals" ([Swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/swiss-oddities/why-switzerland-has-no-head-of-state/90704688); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Federal-Council)) | Bunreacht na hÉireann |

| FM | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FM-01 new class | `0` | `+` no separate executive caste | `+` | `0` |
| FM-02 monopoly | `+` checks | `−−` no independent check on the majority; judges recallable by the majority means dissent goes unprotected | `+` | `+` |
| FM-03 cult | `−` a single elected executive invites personalisation | `+` | `++` collective, rotating executive | `−` Taoiseach dominance |
| FM-04 security | `+` | `−` security under the majority's direct control | `+` | `+` |
| FM-15 emergency | `0` | `−` | `+` courts and integrity bodies review declarations | `0` |
| FM-08 participation | `0` | `+` | `0` | `0` |

**What each costs.**

- **A:** gridlock, and a personalist executive.
- **B:** no protection for minorities or dissidents. The Commune lasted 72 days, so the model was never tested in peacetime.
- **C:**
  - Complexity.
  - The left critique of unelected judges: courts can strike down socialist laws.
  - The integrity bodies need their own accountability.
- **D:** familiar, but executive-dominated.

**Note.** The research leans against B's fusion of the judiciary in particular (FM-02, FM-04). The choice between A, C and D is more open.
