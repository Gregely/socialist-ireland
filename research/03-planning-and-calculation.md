# 03 — Planning, calculation and coordination

## 1. The question

Can an economy without private ownership of the means of production allocate resources well? That means: know what to produce, how, where, with what inputs, and when to stop producing something. The debate has run for a century. The honest summary is that the Austrians won the argument about *centralised Soviet-type planning in practice*. The argument about *democratic, partly decentralised, computer-assisted planning combined with some markets* is still open.

## 2. The calculation debate

### Mises (1920)
- "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth". Without private ownership of producer goods, those goods are not exchanged. Without exchange there are no prices for them. Without such prices, planners cannot compare the costs of alternative uses, so rational calculation is impossible ([mises.org text](https://mises.org/online-book/economic-calculation-socialist-commonwealth/introduction-1990-edition); [overview](https://mises.org/online-book/why-austrian-economics-matters/socialist-calculation)).

### Lange (1936–37)
- "On the Economic Theory of Socialism", *Review of Economic Studies*. A Central Planning Board could set producer prices by **trial and error**: raise them when there are shortages, lower them when there are surpluses. Consumer goods and labour markets would stay free. Lange credited Mises with forcing socialists to take accounting seriously ([Econlib biography](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Lange.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Lange)).

### Hayek (1945)
- "The Use of Knowledge in Society", *American Economic Review*, September 1945 ([Econlib text](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html); [Liberty Fund OLL](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/92)). The core problem is *knowledge*, not computation. Much economically relevant knowledge is *"the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place"*. It is local, tacit and fleeting, and it cannot be passed to a central board. Hayek: *"We must look at the price system as such a mechanism for communicating information if we want to understand its real function"*.
- Hayek moved the debate from a computational problem to an epistemic one, and from equilibrium to process ([Mises Institute summary](https://mises.org/online-book/why-austrian-economics-matters/socialist-calculation)). This is the strongest version of the market critique, and the design must answer it directly, not answer Mises.

### Status of the debate
- Mid-century mainstream economics broadly took Lange to have answered Mises. Later Austrians (from Buchanan in 1969 onward) argued that Lange's model missed the point about knowledge and incentives ([libertarianism.org overview](https://archive.libertarianism.org/topics/socialist-calculation-debate)). Note that this overview is written from the Austrian side.

## 3. Kornai — the soft budget constraint (1980)

- *Economics of Shortage* (1980). Chronic shortage in Soviet-type economies was **systemic**. It was not caused by planners' mistakes or lazy workers. Loss-making state enterprises were never closed. Their budgets were topped up, so their demand for inputs was "almost insatiable". They hoarded materials and labour to make sure they hit plan targets ([Marginal Revolution summary](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2021/10/janos-kornai-and-the-soft-budget-constraint.html); [Kornai obituary](https://uk.news.yahoo.com/j-nos-kornai-obituary-201612161.html)).
- The idea has since been applied well beyond socialism, to "too big to fail" banks and firms ([Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2021/10/janos-kornai-and-the-soft-budget-constraint.html)). The Mercatus Center connects Kornai to the Austrian critique ([Mercatus](https://www.mercatus.org/hayekprogram/research/journal-articles/janos-kornai-austrians-and-political-and-economic-analysis)).
- **Why it matters here.** This is the critique a democratic socialist design is most likely to *underestimate*. Democratic bodies are under even more pressure than Soviet ministries to rescue local employers. Yugoslavia and Mondragon/Fagor (file 06) show versions of the same pressure.

## 4. Nove — *The Economics of Feasible Socialism* (1983)

- Alec Nove was a British economist, the son of exiled Mensheviks, and a leading specialist on the Soviet economy. His goal was a socialism achievable "within the lifetime of a child already conceived" ([Harvard tagteam review](https://tagteam.harvard.edu/hub_feeds/1976/feed_items/8506829); [Labyrinth Books](https://www.labyrinthbooks.com/the-economics-of-feasible-socialism)).
- His model mixes several kinds of ownership: centralised state corporations for natural monopolies and big infrastructure; socialised enterprises with workers' participation; cooperatives; small private businesses; and markets for most goods, with planning for major investment. The core point, quoted in one review, is that *someone (some institution) has to tell the producer about what the users require* ([Dissent](https://dissentmagazine.org/article/david-belkin/)). I have not checked a page reference for this.
- **Critiques.** Ernest Mandel debated Nove in print ("The Myth of Market Socialism"). Chris Harman accused Nove of combining incompatible arguments into "a single onslaught" on democratic planning ([Harman 1989](https://www.marxists.org/archive/harman/1989/xx/marketsoc.html); [marxists.org/svenska](https://www.marxists.org/svenska/nove-a/1983/en_mojlig_socialism.html)).

## 5. Cockshott & Cottrell — *Towards a New Socialism* (1993)

- First published by Spokesman, Nottingham, 1993. It is freely redistributable with attribution ([Glasgow EPUB](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~wpc/reports/tns.epub); [Cottrell's site](https://users.wfu.edu/cottrell/socialism_book/preface-a4.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towards_a_New_Socialism)).
- Their argument combines the labour theory of value, cybernetic coordination and participatory democracy. Modern computers can solve input–output planning problems at national scale. Labour-time accounting could replace money in the planned sector. Consumer goods could be priced by a market-clearing mechanism that signals planners to expand or cut output. Major decisions would be made by direct democracy and sortition ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towards_a_New_Socialism)).
- **The strongest objection.** It answers Mises (computation) better than Hayek (local and tacit knowledge, and incentives to reveal it honestly), and it does not fully address Kornai (incentives). There is also the problem of *heterogeneous* labour: how to value skilled hours against unskilled hours.

## 6. Participatory economics — Albert & Hahnel

- Its institutions are workers' and consumers' councils, **balanced job complexes**, pay according to effort and sacrifice, and decentralised iterative planning. Councils propose, facilitation boards publish updated indicative prices, and the councils revise their proposals until the plan converges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory_economics); [*Looking Forward* glossary](https://znetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/zbooks/www/books/lf_glossary.htm)).
- **Balanced job complexes** bundle tasks within a workplace so that each person's mix is comparable in how empowering and how burdensome it is. The aim is to stop a "coordinator class" monopolising empowering work ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory_economics)). This is a direct response to Djilas and Michels.
- **Critiques.** Meeting overload ("too many meetings"). The burden of iterations. Dilution of expertise. A risk of "facilitatorism": the boards that process the iterations becoming a new coordinator class ([syndicalist.us review](https://syndicalist.us/2012/08/01/book-review-looking-forward/); [Andrew Blackman](https://andrewblackman.net/2008/11/parecon-life-after-capitalism-by-michael-albert/)). Nove-style critics add that iterative planning cannot handle millions of products. The site should say plainly that parecon has never been tried at scale.

## 7. Negotiated coordination — Pat Devine

- *Democracy and Economic Planning* (Polity, 1988). Devine separates *market exchange* (buying and selling current output, which can stay) from *market forces* (decisions about investment and capacity, which should be negotiated). Negotiated coordination bodies bring together the affected interests (workers, users, suppliers, communities, government) to agree on changes in capacity. It assumes that interests differ, and expects people to change their positions through deliberation ([P2P Foundation](https://wiki.P2PFoundation.net/Democracy_and_Economic_Planning); [Anarchist Library review](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/anarcho-review-of-democracy-and-economic-planning)).
- Later developed with Fikret Adaman. It is one of the three standard models of democratic planning, alongside parecon and computerised central planning ([Legault, "Three models"](https://researchgate.net/profile/Frederic-Legault/publication/358834121_A_brief_sketch_of_three_models_of_democratic_economic_planning/links/6217db29efe5b07ebec3028a/A-brief-sketch-of-three-models-of-democratic-economic-planning.pdf)).
- **Critique.** The operational detail is thin. Critics say it is stronger on managing democracy than on managing efficient production ([P2P summary](https://wiki.P2PFoundation.net/Democracy_and_Economic_Planning)).

## 8. Cybersyn — the experimental precedent

- Chile, 1971–73. Stafford Beer's cybernetic management system linked factories by telex to an operations room. During the October 1972 truck-owners' strike, when about 40,000 truck owners stopped work, the telex network helped the government route about 200 loyal vehicles and keep supplies moving ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Cybersyn); [Americas Quarterly](https://americasquarterly.org/article/allendes-strange-plan-to-connect-chile-long-before-the-internet/)). Eden Medina's *Cybernetic Revolutionaries* (MIT Press, 2011) is the standard history ([Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/522475/pdf)).
- **The lesson in both directions.** Real-time information *can* help democratic coordination in a crisis. Medina's account also records the tension between Beer's aim of worker participation and the system's potential to become a tool of top-down control (bactra.org notebook on [Cybersyn](https://bactra.org/notebooks/cybersyn.html)). I have taken this point from reviews and not checked it in the book itself.

## 9. What this suggests for the design

1. **No single planning centre.** Use a layered system:
   - consumer goods and most services through *market exchange* (prices signal demand, as Hayek and Nove argue);
   - major investment and capacity through *negotiated coordination* (Devine);
   - natural monopolies, infrastructure and universal services planned publicly with open data (as Cockshott and Cybersyn suggest);
   - environmental limits set *politically* as binding quantities (caps), not as prices.
2. **Hard budget constraints by default.** Rescuing an enterprise has to be an *explicit, public, voted* decision with a stated cost. It must never be automatic (Kornai).
3. **Open data as infrastructure.** Plan targets, enterprise accounts and input–output tables are published, so that knowledge is not monopolised by planners (an answer to Djilas and Hayek at once).
4. **Honest residual.** The design should say plainly that keeping markets for consumer goods means keeping some inequality from market outcomes, and some of the problems Yugoslavia had (file 06). This is a trade-off, not a solution.
