# 01 — Bureaucratic degeneration, the new class, cults and the security state

## 1. The problem stated

Revolutions made in the name of workers' self-rule produced, within a generation, states run by a stable, privileged administrative stratum. That stratum was answerable to no one below it. Three bodies of theory try to explain this. The trouble is that they come from *inside* the socialist tradition, so there is no "they just weren't real socialists" exit.

## 2. Trotsky — *The Revolution Betrayed* (1936)

- Written in 1936, published in 1937, with the subtitle *What is the Soviet Union and Where is it Going?* Full text at [marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1936/revbet/index.htm).
- **Scarcity is the material root.** Chapter 5, "The Soviet Thermidor": *"The basis of bureaucratic rule is the poverty of society in objects of consumption"*. And: *"When the lines are very long, it is necessary to appoint a policeman to keep order."* The distributor of scarce goods becomes indispensable and then takes its own share first ([ch. 5](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1936/revbet/ch05.htm)).
- **Isolation and backwardness.** The bureaucracy's power grew from Russia's isolation and backwardness after the defeat of revolution elsewhere in Europe ([index and contents](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1936/revbet/index.htm)).
- **"Bonapartism".** Chapter 11 calls the regime a "Bonapartist" balancing act. Policies that look contradictory from a socialist standpoint make sense as strengthening "the new commanding stratum" ([ch. 11](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1936/revbet/ch11.htm)).
- **Prognosis.** Trotsky held this was unstable. It would end either in capitalist restoration *by the bureaucracy itself* or in a political revolution against it (summary at [marxists.org/ETOL](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/document/workersparty/neither-nor/ch1-0-revbetrayed.htm)). The 1989–91 outcome looks a lot like the first branch (see file 05, Kotz & Weir).
- **Criticism of Trotsky.** He still called the USSR a "workers' state" despite the repression, which critics on the left have long disputed ([ISJ review](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/isj2/2016/isj2-151/gordon.html)). Isaac Deutscher called the book a classic, but said some of its ideas "have not stood the test of time equally well" ([Deutscher 1957](https://www.marxists.org/archive/deutscher/1957/revolution-betrayed.htm)). A further critique: Trotsky's own record (Kronstadt, militarisation of labour; see file 02) helped build the machine he later analysed.

**Design lesson.** If scarcity creates the gatekeeper, then (a) universal provision without queues for basics, (b) rule-based and transparent allocation where scarcity remains, and (c) no discretionary allocators who also control their own perks.

## 3. Djilas — *The New Class* (1957)

- Milovan Djilas had been one of Tito's top aides and a vice-president of Yugoslavia. He was expelled in 1954 and imprisoned. Accounts of the dates of imprisonment differ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Class:_An_Analysis_of_the_Communist_System); [Harvard Book Store listing](https://www.harvard.com/book/9780156654890)).
- **Core thesis.** Party-state officials formed a *new class*. They did not legally own nationalised property, but they controlled it, used it and enjoyed it as a collective owner ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Class:_An_Analysis_of_the_Communist_System)).
- **Reception.** Translated into about 50 languages, but banned in Yugoslavia until 1990 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Class:_An_Analysis_of_the_Communist_System)). A Swedish marxists.org introduction says the book was accurate on many features of "socialist" states, but that the "new class" thesis won few Marxist adherents ([marxists.org/svenska](https://www.marxists.org/svenska/djilas-m/1957/den_nya_klassen.html)).

**Design lesson.** Legal ownership is not the point; **control rights** are. Who decides on use, who appoints managers and who takes the surplus matter more than the title deed. Public ownership must be paired with *distributed* control rights, and with open books so that use-rights cannot be quietly privatised.

## 4. Voslensky — *Nomenklatura* (1980)

- Mikhail Voslensky, a Soviet academic who emigrated in 1972, described the nomenklatura as a distinct ruling class that used a system of privileges to keep its dominance ([SPGB review](https://www.worldsocialism.org/spgb/?p=20423); [Ukrainian academic article](https://omp.diit.edu.ua/items/6d789f97-bd43-453e-a9cc-f5dda83f8f33)).
- **Size.** Voslensky's own estimate was about 750,000 people, or about 3 million with families, which is roughly 1.5% of the population (as reported by the [SPGB review](https://www.worldsocialism.org/spgb/?p=20423); I have not checked this against the book itself).
- **Mechanism.** The *nomenklatura* was literally a list of posts. Appointments to them needed party approval, so career advancement depended on loyalty upward and not on accountability downward. Perks came in kind: special shops, extra pay months, health resorts ([SPGB review](https://www.worldsocialism.org/spgb/?p=20423)). The security forces were kept under the Central Committee, which protected the system as a whole ([diit.edu.ua article](https://omp.diit.edu.ua/items/6d789f97-bd43-453e-a9cc-f5dda83f8f33)).
- **Caveat.** Some historians think Voslensky mythologised the nomenklatura and that privilege was spread more widely through Soviet society ([Svensk Tidskrift, 1990](https://www.svensktidskrift.se/arkiv100/1990/18%20Jens%20Knocke%3B%20Nomenklaturan%20och%20realsocialismen.pdf)).

**Design lessons.**

- **No appointment lists controlled by any single body.** Senior posts should be filled by open competition, sortition-vetted panels or election from below, and never by party list.
- **No in-kind perks.** Officials are paid in money at a capped multiple, use the same public services as everyone else, and file public declarations of their assets.

## 5. Michels — the "iron law of oligarchy" (1911)

- Robert Michels's *Political Parties* (1911) studied the German SPD. At the time it was the world's largest socialist party and the one most committed to internal democracy. He found that a small leadership group held the real power and became more conservative over time. He generalised this into an "iron law of oligarchy" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Michels); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/topic/iron_law_of_oligarchy.aspx)).
- **Why it matters here.** Michels shows that degeneration is not only a matter of Russian backwardness. It happens in *democratic, legal, well-resourced* socialist organisations in an advanced country. Expertise, control of information, the organisation's own resources and members' limited time all push power upward.

**Design lesson.** Expect oligarchy as the default and build against it continuously. That means rotation, sharing information, paying for members' time (paid civic leave) and splitting control of an organisation's resources from control of its line.

## 6. The personality cult

- **The record.** Khrushchev's report to the 20th Party Congress (25 February 1956), *On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences*, blamed Stalin's crimes on the cult and on violations of "socialist legality". It largely spared the Party. It was not published in the USSR until 1989 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Cult_of_Personality_and_Its_Consequences)).
- **The critique of Khrushchev's framing.** Blaming the "cult" turns a structural problem into a matter of one man's character. The cult was the *product* of a system in which no rival centre of authority could survive, not its cause. That is my own reading, in line with Trotsky's and Djilas's structural arguments above.
- **The scale of what the cult covered.** Soviet statistics later sent to Khrushchev recorded 681,692 executions by the OGPU-NKVD. Historians' figures for the Great Terror of 1937–38 are similar: Zemskov gives 681,000–692,000 and Werth about 690,000. NKVD Order No. 00447 (30 July 1937) set *quotas* for "first category" (execution) sentences. By the end of 1938, 386,798 people had been executed under that order alone ([Caucasus Analytical Digest](https://ge.boell.org/sites/default/files/uploads/2014/01/caucasusanalyticaldigest22.pdf); [Wikipedia, Order 00447](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NKVD_Order_No._00447); [Werth, via dmitrievaffair.com](https://dmitrievaffair.com/the-great-terror-1937-1938/)). The figures differ because they measure different things; see the sources.

**Design lessons.**

- No individual executive office with independent authority.
- **Collective, rotating chairs.**
- A constitutional ban on naming places, institutions or public holidays after living people. This is my proposal; I have not found a historical precedent and am flagging it as untested.
- A plural public sphere that no single body controls.
- Hard term limits that cannot be changed for the people who currently hold office.

## 7. The security service as a state within the state

- **The Cheka.** Founded in December 1917 under Dzerzhinsky. Sources give 7, 19 and 20 December as the founding date. It was set up to investigate and refer cases to tribunals, but quickly took the power to punish outside the courts. Deputy chair Latsis described it as acting without trial ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Cheka/); [The Russian Reader / Vedomosti](https://therussianreader.com/2017/12/20/how-the-cheka-became-the-fsb/); [AlphaHistory](https://alphahistory.com/russianrevolution/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/110)).
- **Party, not law, was the check.** A 1919 Central Committee decree said the Chekas worked as "direct agencies of the Party" ([The Russian Reader](https://therussianreader.com/2017/12/20/how-the-cheka-became-the-fsb/)). The only check on the security service was the leadership it served. When that leadership turned on its own members in the 1930s, nothing stood in the way.
- **The Stasi as the mature form.** In 1989 the GDR's Stasi had 91,015 full-time staff and 173,081 registered unofficial informants in the GDR. Ratios depend on what is counted: about 1 full-time officer per 166 citizens, and about 1 per 63–66 citizens if informants are included. Estimates that include occasional informants go much higher and are disputed ([Wikipedia, MfS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stasi); [Tel Aviv University](https://www.tau.ac.il/education/muse/maslool/boidem/170stasi.html)).
- **Root causes.** (a) Mission creep from emergency (civil war, sabotage) to permanence. (b) Extrajudicial powers. (c) Secrecy that shuts out oversight. (d) The service answers to a party leadership and not to a plural legislature or courts. (e) Files on everyone, including the officials meant to oversee it.

**Design lessons.**

- Split policing, intelligence and counter-intelligence into separate bodies.
- No powers of arrest or detention for intelligence bodies.
- All coercive powers only under judicial warrant from an independent judiciary.
- Oversight by a body that includes the opposition and sortition members, with full access to the service's records.
- Emergency powers sunset automatically and need supermajority renewal.
- A citizen's right to see one's own file, as the post-1990 German Stasi Records Act provided. That Act is not separately sourced here and needs a source in Phase 2.
- Budgets published by programme.

## 8. Synthesis of this file

| Root cause | Evidence | Counter-mechanism suggested |
|---|---|---|
| Scarcity creates gatekeepers | Trotsky ch. 5 | Universal basic provision; rule-based allocation; no discretionary allocators |
| Control without ownership | Djilas | Distributed control rights; open books |
| Appointment from above | Voslensky | Election or sortition from below; open competition; no party lists |
| In-kind privilege | Voslensky | Cash-only pay at a capped ratio; same services as everyone else |
| Organisational oligarchy | Michels | Rotation; paid civic time; information sharing |
| Cult of personality | Khrushchev 1956 | Collective chairs; no living-person honours; term limits |
| Security service capture | Cheka, Stasi | Separation of powers; warrants; opposition-led oversight; sunset clauses |
