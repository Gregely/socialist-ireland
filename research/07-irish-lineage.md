# 07 — The Irish lineage

The design should root itself in Irish traditions that already exist, not import a foreign template. There are four strands.

---

## 1. William Thompson of Cork (1775–1833) and Ralahine

- **Thompson** was a Cork landowner who became a socialist. His *An Inquiry into the Principles of the Distribution of Wealth Most Conducive to Human Happiness* (London, 1824) makes a utilitarian case against capitalism. He argues that labour is entitled to its whole product and sketches decentralised **cooperative communities** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thompson_(philosopher)); [Irish Marxist Review, "The First Irish Socialist"](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/irishmr/vol01/no04/osullivan.pdf); [Utilitarianism.net](https://www.utilitarianism.net/pdf/William_Thompson_Utilitarianism_net.pdf)). One source dates the *Inquiry* to 1825; 1824 is better supported.
- With **Anna Doyle Wheeler** he wrote *Appeal of One Half the Human Race, Women, Against the Pretensions of the Other Half, Men* (1825). It was a reply to James Mill's argument that only men needed the vote. It argues for women's political, legal *and economic* emancipation, and holds that only association (labour organised through mutual cooperation) could secure real equality between the sexes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thompson_(philosopher)); [EBSCO](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/william-thompson)).
- **Why it matters.** Ireland has a native, pre-Marxian tradition of *cooperative and feminist* socialism. It predates *The Communist Manifesto* by more than twenty years.

### Ralahine, Co. Clare (1831–33)
- John Scott Vandeleur, a landlord influenced by Robert Owen's Dublin lectures, set up a cooperative commune on his estate with the Manchester socialist **Edward Thomas Craig**. Members were paid in **labour notes**, which could be exchanged for coin through Vandeleur. It ran successfully for about two years. In 1833 Vandeleur gambled away the estate and fled, and the commune collapsed. As far as I can tell, its members had no legal claim to the land or capital; this needs checking against Craig's own account ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralahine); [Clare Champion](https://clarechampion.ie/local-group-to-remember-clares-ralahine-commune); [History Ireland](https://www.historyireland.com/?p=30733)). Some historians read Vandeleur's motives as social control, a way to keep tenants out of agrarian secret societies ([Round About the Shannon](https://roundaboutshannon.clareheritage.org/?p=18938)).
- Connolly wrote about Ralahine at length in *Labour in Irish History* ([*Re-Conquest*, citing *Labour in Irish History*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/connolly/1915/rcoi/index.htm)).
- **Lesson.** A cooperative that does not hold legal title to its land and capital is only as secure as its patron. **Collective ownership has to be legally real.**

## 2. James Connolly (1868–1916)

- **"Socialism and Nationalism"**, *Shan Van Vocht*, January 1897. If you *"remove the English army to-morrow and hoist the green flag over Dublin Castle"* without organising a socialist republic, *"England would still rule you to your ruin"* through its capitalists, landlords and financiers ([marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/connolly/1897/01/socnat.htm)). This is the classic Irish statement that political independence without economic change is empty.
- ***Labour in Irish History* (1910).** It rereads Irish history as class struggle. It argues that the Irish middle class was tied economically to English capital, so national independence would bring little freedom to most people unless society's structure changed ([marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/connolly/1910/lih/index.htm); [Hadden](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/hadden/2006/04/connolly.html)).
- ***The Re-Conquest of Ireland* (1915).** Labour *"must set itself the Re-Conquest of Ireland as its final aim"*, meaning *"taking possession of the entire country, all its power of wealth-production and all its natural resources"*, organised on a cooperative basis for the common good ([Foreword, marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/connolly/1915/rcoi/foreword.htm)). Connolly paired this with industrial unionism: the One Big Union as the seed of the future administration (see the pamphlet's later chapters, e.g. [ch. 9](https://www.marxists.org/archive/connolly/1915/rcoi/chap09.htm)).
- **Honest note.** Connolly's views on the national question, religion and the role of a party are argued over by every Irish left tendency, and also by Irish nationalists. The site should present him as a source and not as scripture. His execution in 1916 means we do not know how he would have handled state power.

## 3. The Democratic Programme of the First Dáil (21 January 1919)

- Adopted at the First Dáil's first public sitting alongside the Declaration of Independence. Thomas Johnson of the Labour Party drafted it, with William O'Brien's input. Seán T. O'Kelly then heavily revised it overnight ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Programme); [Century Ireland](https://centuryireland.rte.ie/centuryireland/articles/the-democratic-programme-and-labour-1919)).
- **Key passages** (from the [Wikisource text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Democratic_Programme_of_the_First_D%C3%A1il)):
  - *"the Nation's sovereignty extends not only to all men and women of the Nation, but to all its material possessions"*
  - *"all right to private property must be subordinated to the public right and welfare"*
  - *"declare the right of every citizen to an adequate share of the produce of the Nation's labour"*
  - *"It shall be the first duty of the Government of the Republic to make provision for the physical, mental and spiritual well-being of the children"* (the sentence continues on to hunger, cold and shelter)
  - *"The Irish Republic fully realises the necessity of abolishing the present odious, degrading and foreign Poor Law System"*
- **Fate.** It was never used as a basis for legislation. In April 1919 de Valera said it "contemplated a situation somewhat different" from reality. Kevin O'Higgins later dismissed it as "largely poetry" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Programme)). Emmet O'Connor argues it was drafted mainly to win support for Irish independence at the Berne International Socialist Conference (February 1919), and was not meant to be implemented. His article title is "Neither Democratic nor a Programme" ([*Irish Historical Studies*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/irish-historical-studies/article/abs/neither-democratic-nor-a-programme-the-democratic-programme-of-1919/AD019E7A9447BC1A5F9EABDBD31501B5)).
- **Use in the design.** It is a *founding text of the State itself*. A design that "finally implements the Democratic Programme" has unusual legitimacy in the Irish context. The site should still be honest that the Programme's own authors may not have meant it.

## 4. The soviets of 1919–23

| Date | Place | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 29 Jan – 4 Feb 1919 | Monaghan Asylum | Staff led by Peadar O'Donnell take over the asylum and win a 56-hour week and a pay rise |
| 14–27 Apr 1919 | **Limerick** | General strike against a British "Special Military Area" with a permit regime. The Trades and Labour Council becomes a strike committee, regulates food prices and distribution, controls utilities, and publishes a paper and **its own currency** (Treasurer James Casey) |
| Apr 1920 | Waterford | A strike committee enforces the general strike for hunger strikers |
| 15 May 1920 | **Knocklong**, Co. Limerick | ITGWU workers take over the Cleeve creameries under the banner "Knocklong Soviet Creamery — We Make Butter Not Profits". In five days they win a pay rise, a 48-hour week and 14 days' holidays. The creamery is later burned by the Black and Tans |
| Aug 1921 | Bruree | Cleeve mills and bakery taken over; prices cut |
| 1921–22 | Arigna mines, Drogheda foundry, Castleconnell, Tipperary, Cleeve centres | Further takeovers reported (several poorly documented) |
| 1922–23 | Various | The Free State army arrests leaders and removes red flags. Some soviets were broken up by the IRA. Anti-Treaty forces clashed with soviets in Tipperary |

Sources: [Wikipedia, Irish soviets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_soviets); [Wikipedia, Limerick Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick_Soviet); [History Ireland, Lonergan](https://historyireland.com/the-limerick-soviet-1919); [libcom, "When the red flag flew over Munster"](https://libcom.org/history/when-red-flag-flew-over-munster); Emmet O'Connor, "The Waterford Soviet: Fact or fancy?", *History Ireland* (2000), as cited by Wikipedia.

**Honest notes.**

- Several "soviets" were short industrial disputes that used the soviet *name*. They were not attempts at workers' government. O'Connor's "fact or fancy?" title signals this scepticism.
- Both sides of the Civil War suppressed them. Irish national independence and Irish labour's aims were in conflict at the founding.
- **Lesson.** Workers' councils emerged *spontaneously* in Ireland and ran food supply, prices and money in a city for two weeks. They lacked a unifying structure and armed protection, and they were shut down by nationalists of both sides. The design should name them as ancestors and learn from both their capability and their isolation.
